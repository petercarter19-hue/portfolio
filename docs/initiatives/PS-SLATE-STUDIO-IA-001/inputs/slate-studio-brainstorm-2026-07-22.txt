Peter,
I think this idea is not only good; I think it may be the missing product layer
that finally makes the rest of PeerSlate feel like one coherent system.
Right now, PeerSlate has several strong pieces:

* The public Slate, including the Living Resume, Work, Story, and Ask Pete AI.
* The private Journal and Capture system that preserve what happened.
* Interview Studio, which helps the member practice explaining their experience.
* Slate Board and goals, which help the member think about what comes next.
* Projects, photographs, sources, documents, and evidence.
* Feed and Community, which may eventually distribute selected parts of the
  member's work and growth.

What is missing is the owner's creative workspace in the middle — the place
where all of those materials become something useful.
The Journal remembers. The public Slate presents. Community distributes. But
the member still needs a place to shape, test, organize, improve, and connect
everything. That place should be the Slate Studio.
I would define it this way:
Slate Studio is the member's private career sandbox and professional portfolio
workshop — a creative place to build, update, test, and grow the work, story,
evidence, and direction behind their public Slate.
It is not primarily a resume builder. The resume is one output. It is not
merely a portfolio builder either, because it also includes reflection,
coaching, goals, planning, practice, and future direction. It is the
environment where the member constructs their professional identity from real
evidence and decides what to do with it.
The key is that Slate Studio should not become another giant collection of
pages. It should become the organizing workspace that makes the existing
product easier to understand.

The four-part PeerSlate model
I think PeerSlate becomes much clearer if we establish four simple product
roles:
Journal remembers.
It privately preserves Moments, media, documents, sources, versions, lessons,
and life history.
Slate Studio shapes.
It helps the member turn that material into resume content, Work, Story,
projects, goals, interview examples, and public-ready presentations.
The public Slate presents.
It gives the member one controlled professional link containing the resume,
Work, Story, and Ask [Name] AI.
Community connects and distributes.
It allows selected updates, ideas, progress, and professional-casual expression
to move between people later.
That is the product architecture in language an ordinary user can understand.
It also prevents Capture, Journal, resume, Story, Board, and Community from
competing to be the center of PeerSlate.

Why Slate Studio is not a resume builder
A traditional resume builder begins with a document format. It asks for
employment, dates, education, and bullets, then helps the person produce a PDF.
Slate Studio should begin with the person's professional material and
intentions.
The member may arrive with:

* An existing resume.
* A performance review.
* A project document.
* A photograph.
* A voice note about something that happened.
* A certification.
* A goal.
* A difficult accomplishment they do not know how to explain.
* A Story memory.
* A job description they want to prepare for.
* A desire to make a quick manual correction.

Slate Studio helps them decide what that material is, what it supports, how it
should be expressed, and whether it should remain private or appear somewhere
else.
A resume is therefore one projection of the member's professional record. Work
is another. Story is another. Interview preparation is another. A goal or Feed
post may be another. The same confirmed experience can support several uses
without copying the facts into disconnected systems.
That is a much stronger product than "AI resume builder." It is also much
harder for a generic resume application to imitate because PeerSlate retains
the private history, sources, evidence, context, versions, and relationships
underneath the final wording.

The name
My strongest recommendation is Slate Studio.
"Career Studio" is understandable, but it narrows PeerSlate toward jobs and
advancement. "Portfolio Studio" emphasizes the outward artifact but undersells
goals, reflection, interview practice, and planning. "Work Studio" can sound
like a project-management product. "Career Sandbox" is an excellent internal
description, but "sandbox" may sound technical or temporary to the general
public.
Slate Studio is broad, brand-owned, and premium. It says that this is where the
member makes and refines their Slate.
A user-facing description could be:
Your private workspace for shaping the work, story, proof, and direction behind
your Slate.
Or:
Bring the pieces. Build the picture.
The word "Studio" should describe creativity and active making, not another
dashboard. Interview Studio can remain a named practice experience inside or
adjacent to Slate Studio, although over time I would consider making "Practice"
one of Slate Studio's major modes so that we do not create too many separate
studios.

The Studio should be task-based, not section-heavy
The first screen should not show twelve equal cards for Resume, Work, Skills,
Story, Projects, Media, Goals, Board, Journal, Interview Studio, Feed, and

The Studio Home should instead ask:
What would you like to work on?
Then offer a small number of understandable starting actions:

* Update my Slate.
* Add something new.
* Talk it through with Ask Slate.
* Bring in a document.
* Add photos or work samples.
* Plan what is next.

Below that, the member sees only a few current items:

* Continue the draft they were editing.
* Review two suggestions.
* Finish one incomplete public section.
* Revisit a goal or project.

This makes Slate Studio feel like a creative workspace rather than an

Three primary Studio modes
The overall workspace can be organized into three major modes:
1. Build

identity:

* Identity and introduction.
* Living Resume.
* Work and Projects.
* Skills and evidence.
* My Story.
* Media and supporting artifacts.
* Public Ask [Name] AI knowledge.

The member can edit directly, import, talk to Ask Slate, or draw from Journal.
2. Practice
Practice helps the member communicate and use what they have built:

* Interview Studio.
* Practice explaining a project.
* Turn an achievement into a STAR story.
* Prepare for a manager conversation.
* Practice an introduction or professional summary.
* Compare a resume statement with the fuller evidence behind it.
* Rehearse explaining a career transition.

Interview Studio becomes a powerful module inside the broader professional
workspace instead of feeling like an unrelated public product.
3. Plan
Plan contains the future-facing tools:

* Slate Board.
* Goals.
* Current focus.
* Short-term and long-term direction.
* Skills the member wants to develop.
* Projects they want to complete.
* Career experiments.
* Next actions.

This is where Slate Board can be bundled into the Studio without disappearing.
It becomes the member's planning canvas rather than another major top-level
destination.
Build, Practice, and Plan correspond naturally to the PeerSlate promise:

* Work and Story are built.
* Communication is practiced.
* The Future is planned.

The Journal remains accessible as the source library behind all three, but it
does not need to become another Studio mode.

The owner's four ways to add or update information
Slate Studio should support four equally legitimate methods.
Direct editing
The member knows exactly what to change. They click a bullet, skill, date,
caption, title, Story paragraph, project, goal, or photograph and update it
immediately.
The change saves into a private draft and revision history. It does not require
an AI conversation and does not become public until the member publishes.
Talk it through
The member knows something happened or has an idea but does not know how to
describe it or where it belongs.
Ask Slate begins a short, contextual conversation. It may ask:

* What happened?
* What did you personally do?
* Why did it matter?
* What changed?
* Is there a number, artifact, photograph, or person that supports this?
* Is this something you want to remember privately, add to Work, strengthen a
  resume bullet with, use in an interview, or possibly share?

The conversation produces proposals, not automatic changes.
Upload or import
The member brings in a resume, performance review, award, certification,
project file, presentation, photograph, or other document.
PeerSlate extracts candidate information and shows it in a review tray:

* Confirmed-looking facts.
* Possible resume material.
* Possible Work examples.
* Possible skills.
* Possible Story or Journal context.
* Possible goals or follow-up items.

Every proposed use remains linked to its source. The owner approves, edits,
rejects, or leaves it unplaced.
Use existing material
The member selects from Journal, previous captures, media, Projects, Sources,
or completed goals.
This is important because the Studio should not repeatedly ask members to
recreate material they already own. A button such as Add from my history can
surface relevant Moments and evidence.

Ask Slate should be the Studio collaborator
The private AI should not be a blank chatbot sitting in a corner. It should
operate as a contextual collaborator throughout Slate Studio.
Ask Slate can have four understandable intentions without becoming four
different bots:
Build with me
Help add or structure new information.
Improve this
Review the selected resume bullet, project, Story section, goal, or
introduction.
Find something
Search the member's permitted private history, sources, media, or prior work.
Think this through
Discuss direction, goals, decisions, positioning, or possible next steps.
The AI should always know the active object. If the member is editing a
Northrop Grumman role, Ask Slate should understand that role, its linked
projects, supporting Moments, and the exact bullet being discussed. The member
should not have to paste everything into a generic chat window.
The conversation should also be visibly grounded. PeerSlate should show which
facts came from the member, which sources support them, what wording was
AI-proposed, and what remains uncertain.

The idea of a real sandbox
The word "sandbox" can become more than a metaphor. Slate Studio should allow
the member to experiment without affecting the live Slate.
A member might want to:

* Try three versions of a professional summary.
* See whether a project should be featured under Work or the resume.
* Compare a concise bullet with a more detailed version.
* Explore how to describe a career transition.
* Build a leadership-oriented version of an introduction.
* Test a Story structure.
* Prepare material for a particular conversation or opportunity.

These experiments should occur in private draft branches or "working versions."
The underlying canonical facts remain shared, but the presentation wording can
differ.
I would not immediately turn this into multiple public resumes or complex
audience profiles. Initially, the member simply needs a safe working state
with:

* Current live version.
* Private draft.
* One or more proposed alternatives.
* Compare changes.
* Apply selected changes.
* Discard without consequence.

Later, PeerSlate could support temporary "lenses" for approved uses, such as a
leadership conversation or a particular opportunity. Those lenses should be
projections of the same history, not new truth stores.

A creative but controlled visual direction
Slate Studio should feel more like an editorial workspace or creative workbench
than a conventional SaaS dashboard.
The current Deep Navy and warm ivory PeerSlate world can support this
beautifully.
The layout might use:
The Workbench
A large central area containing the one object the member is actively shaping:
a resume role, Work project, Story section, goal, or photograph.
The Material Tray
A quiet rail containing recent captures, uploaded documents, photographs,
unplaced Moments, and AI suggestions that may be useful. The tray should never
imply that every item requires action.
The Ask Slate panel
A contextual right rail for feedback, conversation, evidence questions, and
proposed revisions.
The Live Slate preview
A persistent but restrained way to switch from the private working view to the
exact public result. The member should always understand what is private, what
is drafted, and what is currently live.
The Direction Board
The existing Slate Board can become the Plan canvas inside the Studio. It can
retain the tactile whiteboard character while using the same owner shell and
canonical goals.
The Shelf
Projects, media, documents, credentials, and finished artifacts can feel like a
member's professional shelf or library. This should be an organizational
metaphor, not a photoreal room or gimmicky skeuomorphism.
The fun should come from movement, composition, discovery, and satisfying
progress — not badges, streaks, confetti, or pressure.
Examples of pleasant interactions include:

* Dragging a selected Work item into a role's featured area.
* Seeing a photograph become a Story card or project cover.
* Watching a vague capture resolve into a clear Moment and several optional
  uses.
* Comparing before-and-after wording.
* Pulling a past accomplishment from Journal into interview practice.
* Marking a goal complete and deciding whether it should become a private
  Moment, Work update, or public milestone.

Goals and Slate Board integration
I do think Slate Board should be bundled into this concept, but it should keep
a distinct personality.
Slate Board becomes the Plan surface within Slate Studio. It can hold:

* Current focus.
* Near-term goals.
* Longer-term direction.
* Work and personal-professional projects.
* Skills to develop.
* Upcoming conversations or milestones.
* Ideas that are not ready for commitment.

The powerful part is how goals connect to the rest of PeerSlate.
A goal can link to:

* A skill.
* A project.
* An Interview Studio practice plan.
* A future Work item.
* A Story direction.
* Journal Moments that show progress.

When a goal changes or is completed, PeerSlate can ask:

* Save this progress to Journal?
* Update the public current-focus area?
* Add this as a milestone?
* Practice explaining what you learned?
* Share a selected update?

Again, these are choices, not automatic publication.
This makes Slate Board more than a task board. It becomes the future-facing
part of the member's professional identity system.

Photos, media, and evidence
Slate Studio should make visual material easy and enjoyable to use.
The member should be able to:

* Upload directly from the active project or Story section.
* Choose an existing asset from Journal's Photos & Video view.
* Add a photograph while talking to Ask Slate.
* Attach a document, screenshot, presentation, video, award, or credential.
* Save media privately without deciding where it belongs.

Each item can later be used in Work, Story, a project, a Feed post, or nowhere
public.
For professional Work, evidence does not have to be publicly downloadable. A
restricted or sensitive artifact can still exist as a private source that helps
the member remember and describe what they did without exposing the artifact
itself. PeerSlate must preserve those boundaries, particularly for members
working in defense, healthcare, government, or other sensitive environments.

A possible Slate Studio home
The top of the Studio might say:
What would you like to shape today?
Then provide four primary actions:

* Edit my Slate.
* Talk with Ask Slate.
* Add material.
* Plan what is next.

Below that:
Continue working
One or two active drafts.
Worth a look
A small number of optional suggestions, such as an unplaced accomplishment, an
incomplete project, or a Moment that could support a resume bullet.
Your live Slate
A thumbnail or quiet status showing the current published state and the last
update, with Preview and Edit actions.
Current direction
One goal or focus from Slate Board.
That is enough. It should not become a data-heavy dashboard with counts,
charts, activity metrics, or dozens of modules.

The onboarding experience
Slate Studio should be introduced during onboarding as the place the member
will return to maintain everything.
The member can learn the system through a short sequence:

1. Claim a Slate link.
2. Upload a resume or begin manually.
3. Make one direct change.
4. Talk through one meaningful experience by voice or text.
5. Review how PeerSlate can use that experience.
6. Add one photograph, document, or Work item.
7. Set one current goal or direction if desired.
8. Preview the Slate.
9. Publish deliberately.

This teaches that PeerSlate accepts information in several ways. The user never
concludes that they must always speak to AI, and they also do not miss the
value of Capture and conversation.

The public Slate should remain simple
Slate Studio may become rich, but the public Slate must remain calm.
The owner can have hundreds of Moments, documents, drafts, goals, versions, and
possible uses. The visitor should see only a curated professional identity:

* Slate Cover.
* Living Resume.
* Selected Work.
* My Story.
* Ask [Name] AI.
* Contact.

Slate Studio exists partly to protect that simplicity. It handles the
complexity privately so the public result can remain coherent.

* Slate Studio.

Capture remains a universal action.
Inside Slate Studio:

* Build.
* Practice.
* Plan.

Interview Studio lives under Practice. Slate Board and Goals live under Plan.
Resume, Work, Story, Projects, and public AI configuration live under Build.
This is a substantial simplification. It does not remove functionality. It

Roadmap integration
This should become one umbrella initiative, not a random new package added
after the others.
Strategic amendment
Add Slate Studio as the owner composition and growth layer between Journal and
the public Slate.
Phase 1: Product hierarchy and information architecture
Define the four-part model: Journal remembers, Slate Studio shapes, the public

Phase 4: Owner shell

Community phases
Community should consume deliberate public or audience-limited placements from

Recommended work packages
I would structure this as the following controlled packages:
PS-SLATE-STUDIO-IA-001
Defines the complete Studio information architecture, Build/Practice/Plan
model, owner navigation, and relationship to Journal and the public Slate.
PS-SLATE-STUDIO-SHELL-001
Creates the workbench, Material Tray, contextual Ask Slate panel, live preview,
drafts, and responsive owner shell.
PS-SLATE-STUDIO-BUILD-001
Integrates resume, Work, Story, Projects, skills, media, direct editing,
import, and evidence.
PS-SLATE-STUDIO-COACH-001
Implements contextual conversation, follow-up questions, proposed wording,
comparisons, source grounding, and approve/reject behavior.
PS-SLATE-STUDIO-PRACTICE-001
Connects Interview Studio and communication practice to actual resume, Work,
Story, and Journal material.
PS-SLATE-STUDIO-PLAN-001
Integrates Slate Board, goals, current focus, progress Moments, and future
direction.
PS-SLATE-STUDIO-ONBOARD-001
Creates the first-run experience demonstrating direct editing, Capture, upload,
media, goals, preview, and publication.
These should share one architecture and design gate. They should not be
designed as seven unrelated products.

Before runtime work, I would create production-intent designs for:

1. Slate Studio Home.
2. Build mode with a resume role open.
3. Direct bullet editing.
4. Ask Slate coaching on that bullet.
5. Uploading and reviewing a performance document.
6. Turning a Capture into a Work candidate.

Pete and Danielle should both be used as founding-alpha cases because their

What not to do
Do not create a huge professional dashboard containing every feature.
Do not make AI the only way to edit.
Do not force every change into Journal.
Do not turn Slate Studio into a job-search portal.
Do not build a general project-management product.
Do not make goals public by default.
Do not create multiple AI personalities for resume, Story, goals, and projects.
Do not duplicate canonical information across resume, Work, Story, and
Practice.
Do not let the richness of the private Studio make the public Slate more
complicated.
Do not use game mechanics that create pressure, shame, or performative
productivity.

Final recommendation
I think Slate Studio should become one of PeerSlate's defining concepts.
The public Slate is why someone understands and shares the product. Journal and
Capture are why the member can retain their real history. Slate Studio is why
they can actually build with that history and keep coming back.
This may resolve several strategic problems at once:

* It gives direct editing a natural home.
* It gives Ask Slate a meaningful private role.
* It keeps Capture important without making it mandatory.
* It connects resume, Work, Story, Projects, media, and evidence.
* It gives Interview Studio a relationship to the member's actual history.
* It gives Slate Board and goals a coherent place.
* It makes onboarding outcome-driven and creative.
* It protects the public Slate from becoming overloaded.
* It provides recurring value without relying first on Community or addictive
  engagement.

Most importantly, it makes PeerSlate feel less like a collection of impressive
features and more like a complete system.
The simplest expression is:
Journal remembers. Slate Studio shapes. Your Slate presents. Community
connects.
That is a product people can understand, and it gives the roadmap a stable
structure for deciding where every current and future idea belongs.

THE MERGE SYSTEM — HOW TO STOCKPILE NOW AND NARROW LATER

You're about to have three piles (yours, mine, ChatGPT's) and they WILL
overlap — mirrors, recaps, and prompt decks are convergent ideas everyone
reaches. Two rules make the merge painless:

Rule 1 — merge by job, not by name. Every idea gets one index card, same
format:

  Name · Wing (Work / Story / Future / Center / Drop Zone) · What you do, in
  ten words · What it leaves in your pocket (the optional artifact) · What it
  needs (live today / register-designed / new infra)

Two cards with the same "what you do" + same "what it leaves" are THE SAME
IDEA wearing different names — keep the best name, note it got two votes. And
that's the reframe on your tally: when your pile and chat's pile land on the
same toy independently, that's not competition for credit — convergence is
validation. The ideas that show up in every pile unprompted are the ones the
product is trying to tell you it wants.

Rule 2 — narrow with four tests, in this order:

1. The Tuesday test — would anyone touch it on a random Tuesday with no
   agenda? (Kills dashboard-ware.)
2. The grounded test — does it get meaningfully better because it knows YOUR

   wrapper, not a room. (Kills the copyable stuff — this is your moat test.)
3. The pocket test — does play leave an optional artifact behind — a Moment, a
   goal, material? (Kills the arcade games; keeps the compounding ones.)
4. The plumbing test — live today / already designed in your register / needs
   new infrastructure? (Sorts survivors into waves — this one SEQUENCES, it
   doesn't kill.)

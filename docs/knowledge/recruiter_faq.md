Problem Solving and Systems Thinking

How does Pete approach a challenging problem?

Pete begins by defining the problem and making sure he understands what is actually happening rather than reacting only to the first symptom.

He identifies the affected system, stakeholders, constraints, dependencies, risks, available data, and desired outcome. When a problem is complex, he breaks it into smaller elements and researches each one.

He then develops potential solutions and evaluates them based on factors such as:

* Technical feasibility
* Safety
* Cost
* Schedule
* Risk
* Return on investment
* Resource availability
* Supportability
* Long-term effectiveness
* Customer and mission impact

Pete discusses the options with the relevant stakeholders, selects or recommends the most practical solution, implements it, and monitors the results. When necessary, he adjusts the solution based on testing, feedback, or changing conditions.

⸻

How does Pete use systems thinking?

Pete evaluates problems within the context of the entire system rather than focusing only on one component or organization.

For example, a missing part may appear to be a supply issue, but the real solution may involve repair procedures, technical data, testing equipment, engineering authority, a substitute part, depot workload, contractor performance, configuration requirements, or aircraft divestment plans.

Pete considers how technical decisions affect production, supply, maintenance, cost, schedule, safety, readiness, contracts, customers, and long-term sustainment.

His background across engineering, logistics, contracting, project management, depot operations, and requirements helps him identify connections that may be missed when organizations work independently.

⸻

How does Pete evaluate multiple technical or project options?

Pete first identifies the realistic options and gathers enough information to understand each one.

He then evaluates:

* Whether the option is technically feasible
* Whether it meets requirements and applicable standards
* The expected cost
* The implementation schedule
* Technical and programmatic risks
* The expected benefit
* The return on investment
* Long-term maintenance and sustainment effects
* Stakeholder concerns
* Safety and user impact

He discusses the options with the appropriate technical and program stakeholders before recommending or selecting a path.

Once a decision is made, he helps implement the solution and monitors whether it produces the intended result.

Examples of Technical Problem Solving

Can you provide an example of Pete solving a difficult sustainment problem?

Pete supported a system with more than 20 assets that had remained in Awaiting Parts status for more than two years. Most of the cases were associated with shortages of a power supply and an oscillator.

An initial solution involved bringing in additional assets and removing components for reuse, but progress was slow. During a later depot visit, Pete initiated an unplanned working session with the scheduler, planner, and technician to reconsider the approach.

The team developed a broader cross-cannibalization and reclamation strategy. The plan involved bringing in additional donor assets, removing the remaining Awaiting Parts units, redistributing available power supplies and oscillators, reclaiming usable components, and condemning units that could no longer be economically restored.

The planned outcome was at least ten additional serviceable assets and an estimated one to two years of additional sustainment coverage for the system.

This example demonstrates Pete’s willingness to revisit an ineffective approach, engage the people closest to the work, and develop a solution based on the full asset posture rather than handling each unit independently.

⸻

Can you describe a major end-of-life challenge Pete worked on?

Pete learned that the ARN-108 and ARN-112 systems had end-of-life concerns that had been documented years earlier without a complete replacement or sustainment solution. At the time, sustainment was projected to become increasingly difficult by the end of 2025.

He researched the systems, asset posture, demand, available inventory, potential replacements, and aircraft usage. He increased engagement with depot supervisors, planners, and technicians and began including them in recurring Integrated Product Team discussions.

Pete also helped a newer program manager develop a coordinated plan involving multiple aircraft program offices, including stakeholders associated with the F-15, F-16, A-10, and B-1. The team gathered information about installed quantities, aircraft-divestment plans, equipment removal schedules, and potential sources of reclaimable assets.

Pete also researched a possible replacement system and communicated with an industry representative to evaluate compatibility across aircraft platforms.

Through cross-cannibalization, use of serviceable components, and planning for future aircraft-regeneration and storage-facility pulls, the team identified ways to extend sustainment while the Air Force continued pursuing a long-term replacement.

Can you provide an example of Pete resolving a production work stoppage?

Pete worked with engineering and depot personnel to address an Automatic Direction Finder test limitation that had caused a production work stoppage lasting approximately four months.

The existing tester could not support the required prolonged testing. Pete collaborated with the responsible engineer and depot stakeholders to evaluate the limitation and identify an alternate test approach.

A revised procedure was implemented that allowed technicians to complete acceptable testing without relying on the unavailable capability. This allowed production to resume and reduced the risk of future backorders and mission-impairment conditions.

Can you provide an example of Pete identifying an unnecessary engineering project?

Pete frequently worked with depot technicians and other stakeholders to investigate Part Number Supportability Review submissions.

In several cases, direct research and discussion showed that the identified component, submission, or obsolescence assumption was incorrect. This contributed to cancellation of three planned engineering projects with a combined estimated value greater than $2 million.

In another case, Pete determined that a request for an APS-133 component had been submitted for the wrong part. The request was cancelled, and the program used available next-higher assemblies to meet the actual demand.

These examples show the value of validating the problem before committing significant time and funding to a proposed solution.

Can you provide an example of Pete protecting technical requirements during a project?

During the ARN-67 redesign effort, supply-chain problems caused the contractor to consider components that were expected to fall approximately five degrees below the applicable military-standard temperature requirement.

Initial analysis suggested that the lower component rating might not affect system-level performance. However, Pete was not comfortable accepting the added risk without exhausting other options.

He arranged an in-person meeting at the contractor’s facility and asked the team to reconsider earlier design configurations that had been changed because of prior supply issues.

The contractor researched those configurations and determined that components for one of the previous designs had become available. The project moved forward using parts that met the applicable military requirements, without requiring an additional schedule extension.

The result reduced technical and user risk while avoiding further cost and schedule growth.

Conflict and Difficult Conversations

How does Pete handle conflict?

Pete tries to address conflict directly but thoughtfully.

He first takes time to understand whether the disagreement is technical, procedural, interpersonal, or caused by incomplete information. He then speaks with the person involved, asks questions, explains his perspective, and focuses the conversation on the shared objective.

He does not expect every disagreement to end with one person fully accepting the other’s position. A successful outcome may be a better understanding, a workable compromise, or a clear decision that allows the team to continue productively.

⸻

Can you provide an example of Pete resolving conflict with a coworker?

A new program manager once asked Pete for an update on an oscillator issue. Pete provided the current status along with historical context and prior options the team had evaluated.

The program manager responded in a group email that she wanted only the current update and did not need a history lesson. Pete found the response unnecessarily harsh.

Rather than replying emotionally, Pete waited until he had considered how to approach the situation. He then spoke with the program manager directly and asked why she had reacted that way.

She explained that the historical information felt as though Pete was telling her how the previous program manager had handled the work and, indirectly, how she should manage it.

Pete clarified that his intention was to help the team make informed decisions and emphasized that they needed to work together. Although the conversation did not include a formal apology, it created a better mutual understanding, and the same type of conflict did not continue.

⸻

How did Pete handle a disagreement with management or a subject-matter expert?

While developing a new tracking tool, Pete and a subject-matter expert disagreed about how users should update the system.

Pete recommended that each engineer use an individual copy, with data consolidated automatically, because the shared tracker could be opened by only one user at a time. He was concerned that users waiting until the deadline would be locked out.

The subject-matter expert preferred a single shared tracker because it minimized version-control and data-transfer risks.

They discussed the advantages and risks of both approaches. The final solution kept one authoritative tracker but moved the internal deadline forward by one week, providing additional time for updates and reducing the risk created by temporary access conflicts.

The outcome preserved configuration control while addressing Pete’s schedule concern.

Can you provide an example of Pete dealing with an underperforming teammate?

Pete worked with a new supply planner who frequently missed meetings, did not respond consistently, and was not completing actions needed by the Integrated Product Team.

Pete initially increased the frequency of his follow-ups, but the improvement was inconsistent. When the organization began returning to the office periodically, Pete invited the planner to join him on a depot visit.

During the visit, Pete learned that the planner felt overwhelmed and had received inadequate training. Pete explained that the team could help, but only if the planner communicated when he did not understand something or needed support.

Pete then raised the underlying training problem with the program manager, who connected the employee with a highly experienced supply planner for mentoring.

The employee’s communication and productivity improved substantially, and the overall team began completing work more effectively.

This experience reinforced Pete’s belief that apparent performance problems may be symptoms of training, confidence, workload, or communication issues rather than a lack of effort.

Mistakes, Failure, and Accountability

Can you provide an example of a mistake Pete made?

Early in his systems-engineering career, Pete’s supervisor asked him about a system experiencing sustainment problems. During the discussion, Pete realized that he had confused it with another assigned system and had provided incorrect information.

He immediately called the supervisor back, acknowledged the mistake, apologized, and corrected the information.

Pete then addressed the underlying cause. He created a detailed Excel parts and systems tracker containing system names, National Stock Numbers, part numbers, next-higher assemblies, aircraft platforms, technical orders, product-information links, manufacturers, notes, and overall asset posture.

The tracker became his primary reference tool, significantly improving the speed and accuracy of his research. Other engineers later developed their own versions after seeing how he used it.

The recovery demonstrated accountability and converted an individual mistake into a stronger repeatable process.

He identifies which tasks directly affect customers, production, senior-leadership commitments, system readiness, contract performance, or other team members’ ability to proceed.
